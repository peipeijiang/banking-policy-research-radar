<summary><strong>中文摘要</strong></summary>

泰国向高收入地位的转型，其核心在于城市挑战。曼谷贡献了全国近一半的国内生产总值，但其主导地位日益受到拥堵、环境压力以及进一步集聚的边际收益递减的制约。历次国家空间发展战略均呼吁构建更为多节点的城市层级体系，但评估这一雄心的分析基础仍然薄弱。本文构建并校准了一个适用于2025至2050年泰国城市体系的动态递归空间量化一般均衡模型，并利用该模型评估三套反事实政策组合，这些组合将相同的总生产率投资规模（约为2030年城市国内生产总值的2.3%）以不同比例分配至曼谷与十一个指定次级城市：偏向曼谷的组合（80/20）、均衡组合（50/50）以及偏向次级城市的组合（20/80）。偏向曼谷的组合在2045年之前保持领先，但偏向次级城市的组合在2045至2050年间实现反超，其带来的全国人均国内生产总值较无投资基准情景高出12.38%，而偏向曼谷的替代方案则为11.62%。均衡组合在2050年被两种偏斜方案同时支配，这反映了集聚经济的阈值特性。通过二维的投资规模—分配比例扫描分析表明，相对排序确实取决于承诺的规模与时间跨度：低于某一临界点时，偏向曼谷的组合占优；高于该临界点时，偏向次级城市的组合占优。一个适度且配置合理的空间投资计划不太可能成为泰国高收入转型的引擎；足够的承诺规模是前提条件。

<summary><strong>英文摘要</strong></summary>

Thailand's transition to high-income status is, at its core, an urban challenge. Bangkok generates nearly half of the national gross domestic product, yet its dominance is increasingly constrained by congestion, environmental stress, and diminishing returns to further concentration. Successive National Spatial Development Strategies have called for a more multi-nodal urban hierarchy, but the analytical basis for evaluating that ambition has remained thin. This paper builds and calibrates a Dynamic Recursive Spatial Quantitative General Equilibrium model for Thailand's urban system from 2025 to 2050, and uses it to evaluate three counterfactual policy portfolios that allocate the same aggregate productivity-investment envelope (roughly 2.3 percent of 2030 urban gross domestic product) across Bangkok and the eleven designated secondary cities in different proportions: a Bangkok-leaning portfolio (80/20), a balanced portfolio (50/50), and a secondary-leaning portfolio (20/80). The Bangkok-leaning portfolio leads through 2045, but the secondary-leaning portfolio overtakes it between 2045 and 2050, delivering a 12.38 percent gain in national gross domestic product per capita above the no-investment baseline against 11.62 percent for the Bangkok-leaning alternative. The balanced portfolio is dominated by both skewed alternatives in 2050, reflecting a threshold property of agglomeration economies. A two-dimensional envelope-by-split sweep shows that the relative ranking is genuinely contingent on the scale and horizon of the commitment: below a certain tipping point, the Bangkok-leaning portfolio dominates; above it, the secondary-leaning portfolio does. A modest, well-allocated spatial-investment program is unlikely to be the engine of Thailand's high-income transition; sufficient scale of commitment is a precondition.
