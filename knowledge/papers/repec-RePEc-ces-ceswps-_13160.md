# Degrees for GPTs: The Effects of Generative AI on Skill Demand and Supply

[查看原文](https://ideas.repec.org/p/ces/ceswps/_13160.html)

## 一句话结论

> 利用德州公立大学行政记录与招聘数据，将AI自动化暴露度映射到大学专业，发现ChatGPT发布后高暴露专业毕业生首年收入下降约10%，并转向读研但未获补偿，低年级学生随后调整专业选择。

<summary><strong>中文摘要</strong></summary>

我们考察生成式人工智能（generative AI）的快速部署如何影响应届大学毕业生的劳动力市场结果和教育决策。利用将德克萨斯州公立四年制大学所有学生与州收入数据相关联的行政记录，以及详细的职位招聘数据，我们通过将职业任务指标映射到大学专业来衡量人工智能自动化暴露度。我们发现，在ChatGPT于2022年11月发布后，来自人工智能自动化暴露度高出10个百分点的专业的毕业生，其首年收入降低了10%，这既源于就业率下降，也源于条件收入降低。面对疲软的劳动力需求，来自暴露度较高专业的毕业生更有可能进入研究生院就读。然而，他们大多继续学习同一领域，而暴露度较高领域新近硕士毕业生的收入下降表明，研究生层次的教育未能释放足够大的互补性，以抵消这些领域的“自动化惩罚”。相比之下，来自暴露专业但课程偏向低暴露领域的学生，其收入损失较小。本科入学数据显示，截至2025年秋季，较年轻的学生正在从人工智能暴露领域转向其他方向，这与先前行业冲击对专业选择的延迟效应相一致。

<summary><strong>英文摘要</strong></summary>

We examine how the rapid deployment of generative AI affects labor market outcomes and educational decisions of recent college graduates and current students. Using administrative records that link all students at Texas public four-year universities to state earnings data, along with detailed job postings data, we measure exposure to AI automation by mapping occupational task measures onto college majors. We find that graduates from majors with 10 percentage points higher exposure to AI automation experienced 10% lower first-year earnings following ChatGPT's release in November 2022, driven by both reduced employment rates and lower conditional earnings. In response to soft labor demand, graduates from more-exposed majors were more likely to enroll in graduate school. However, they largely continued studying the same field, and earnings declines for recent master's recipients in more-exposed fields suggest that graduate-level education does not unlock complementarities large enough to offset "automation penalties" in these fields. In contrast, graduates from exposed majors whose coursework tilted toward less-exposed fields experienced smaller earnings losses. Undergraduate enrollment data shows that younger students are pivoting away from AI-exposed fields as of Fall 2025, consistent with the delayed effects of prior sectoral shocks on major choice.
