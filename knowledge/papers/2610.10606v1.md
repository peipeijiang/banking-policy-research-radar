# The Gendered Impacts of Perceived Skin Tone: Evidence from African American Siblings in 1870-1940

[查看原文](http://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10606v1)

## 一句话结论

> 利用1870-1940年美国人口普查数据，通过同性别兄弟姐妹的肤色分类差异，识别感知肤色对非裔美国人教育、婚姻和收入的影响，发现女性受歧视更严重，男性差异主要源于家庭机会差异。

<summary><strong>中文摘要</strong></summary>

我们利用19世纪末和20世纪初美国十年一次的人口普查全计数数据，研究非裔美国人中因感知肤色不同而导致的经济结果差异。通过比较人口普查员标记为“Black”或“Mulatto”的儿童，并将这些儿童在历次人口普查中进行关联，我们首先记录了肤色感知较深和较浅的非裔美国人在教育程度和收入方面存在的巨大差距。为厘清这些差距的驱动因素，我们识别出所有35,992个家庭，在这些家庭中，普查员将同性别兄弟姐妹分配了不同的Black/Mulatto分类。相对于被标记为Mulatto的姐妹，被标记为Black的姐妹教育程度更低，结婚可能性更小，且其丈夫受教育程度更低、收入可能也更低。这些模式与当时对感知肤色较深的非裔美国女性更为严重的歧视相一致。相比之下，我们发现被不同分类的兄弟在教育程度、婚姻结果和收入方面相似。被视为任何肤色的非裔美国男性都面临相似的当代歧视，这与“一滴血”种族分类规则一致，该规则将任何已知有黑人血统的个体归为一类。在一般人群中，被视为肤色较深的非裔美国男性收入较低，这是由因家庭而异的机会和资源差异所驱动的，可能反映了历史或家庭层面歧视的影响。

<summary><strong>英文摘要</strong></summary>

We study differences in economic outcomes by perceived skin tone among African Americans using full-count U.S. decennial census data from the late-19th and early-20th centuries. Comparing children coded as "Black" or "Mulatto" by census enumerators and linking these children across population censuses, we first document large gaps in educational attainment and income among African Americans with darker and lighter perceived skin tones. To disentangle the drivers of these gaps, we identify all 35,992 families in which enumerators assigned same-gender siblings different Black/Mulatto classifications. Relative to sisters coded as Mulatto, sisters coded as Black had lower educational attainment, were less likely to marry, and had less-educated, likely lower-earning husbands. These patterns are consistent with more severe contemporaneous discrimination against African American women with darker perceived skin tones. In contrast, we find similar educational attainment, marital outcomes, and incomes among differently-classified brothers. Men perceived as African Americans of any skin tone faced similar contemporaneous discrimination, consistent with the "one-drop" racial classification rule that grouped together individuals with any known Black ancestry. Lower incomes for African American men perceived as having darker skin tone in the general population were driven by differences in opportunities and resources that varied across families, likely reflecting the impacts of historical or family-level discrimination.
